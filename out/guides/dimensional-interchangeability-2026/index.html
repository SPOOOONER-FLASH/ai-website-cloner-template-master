Dimensional Interchangeability 2026: The Seven Dimensions That Decide Whether a Lock Drops In, and How Many We Publish

"Is it interchangeable?" is answered by seven dimensions, and two of them are the ones almost nobody publishes. This page lists them in the order they cause failures, explains why matching the backset is not enough, sets out what to send a factory when you want a drop-in for a discontinued lock, and counts how many of our own 258 lock products publish each dimension, including the one at two per cent.

A buyer asks whether a lock is interchangeable with one they already buy. The answer is never yes or no; it is a list of seven dimensions, and the honest reply names which ones match, which do not, and which nobody has measured.

The order below is the order in which they cause failures on site, which is not the order in which people check them.

The seven, in order of what they cost

The pattern in that table is worth stating plainly: the expensive failures are near the top, and the top two are the two people check least.

Why matching the backset is not enough

The backset is the dimension everybody knows, and it is the one that gets quoted in an inquiry as though it settled the question. It does not, because it locates only one hole.

On a mortise lock, the backset places the spindle relative to the door edge. The center distance places the cylinder relative to the spindle. Two locks can share a 60mm backset and have different center distances, and a door prepped for one has its cylinder hole in the wrong place for the other.

That is not a minor mismatch. The spindle hole and the cylinder hole are both through the face of the door; moving one is not possible, and a cover plate over a hole in the middle of a door face is not an outcome anybody accepts.

Our backset and center distance page covers the two dimensions in detail. For an interchangeability question, the rule is simply that they are two questions and both have to be asked.

The forend is the dimension nobody measures

The forend (the faceplate on the door edge) has three properties: length, width, and whether its corners are square or radiused.

It does not affect whether the lock works. It entirely decides whether the installation looks like it was planned. A replacement lock with a shorter forend leaves the old mortise visible above and below the new plate, on every door in the building, at eye level for anybody standing at it.

The corner radius matters for the same reason it matters on a strike plate: a mortise cut with a router has radiused corners, one cut with a chisel has square ones, and the mismatch shows as four crescents or four squares.

This is the dimension most often discovered after the first door is fitted, which is also the point at which the decision to proceed has already been made.

How much of this we actually publish

Interchangeability is a question about published dimensions, so the honest thing is to say how many we publish. Counted from the specification rows of the 258 products in our catalog whose category or name identifies them as locks, latches, cylinders or deadbolts.

Those last three rows are the point of this page. The two dimensions that cause the most expensive failures (center distance and forend) are the two we publish least, and case size appears on only four of them.

We would rather print that table than describe our data as comprehensive. A buyer who knows the gap can ask for the measurement; a buyer who assumes the catalog is complete finds out after cutting.

Where a dimension is missing, it is missing because nobody has measured that model to a drawing yet, not because it is withheld. Ask for the model you need and it can be measured; that is a short conversation, and much shorter than the one after a container of doors.

What to send when you want a drop-in for a lock you already buy

A sample is worth more than a photograph and far more than a description. Failing that, send these, in this order.

The existing lock's make and model number, even if the brand is one we do not stock. A model number lets us look for published data before anybody measures anything.

Photographs of the door edge with the lock in place, and of the door face, each with a ruler or caliper in frame so scale is readable.

The seven dimensions above, measured, with a note of which you measured and which you copied from a document. That distinction matters, our drawings page explains why a dimension you measured is your measurement until a factory confirms it in writing.

The door thickness, and whether the door is timber, hollow metal or a composite, because a 45mm timber leaf and a 45mm hollow metal leaf take fixings differently.

The finish and the function, because a drop-in that is dimensionally perfect and functionally wrong is still a rejected delivery.

Measuring an installed lock without taking the door off

Most interchangeability questions are asked about a lock that is already in service, in a building that cannot be shut while somebody measures it. All seven dimensions can be taken with a caliper, a steel rule and about ten minutes per door type, and three of them do not require removing anything.

Backset, without removal: measure from the face of the forend to the center of the spindle, along the door edge. The spindle is visible where it passes through the case, and its center is the same center the handle turns on, so measuring from the door edge to the middle of the handle's rose, on the face of the door, gives the same number and is easier to do accurately.

Center distance, without removal: measure between the center of the spindle and the center of the cylinder on the face of the door. Both are visible. This is the dimension that decides whether a door can be reused, and it costs nothing to take.

Forend length and width, without removal: measure the visible plate on the door edge, and look at its corners. Photograph them against the rule rather than describing them, "radiused" without a radius is not a dimension.

The remaining four need the lock out, or a drawing. Case size and fixing centers are inside the door. Spindle square can be measured on the spindle itself if a handle is removed, which is two screws rather than a whole lock. Door thickness range is a property of the lock rather than the door, so it comes from the manufacturer, but measure the door itself while you are there, at the lock edge, with a caliper rather than a tape, because a tape across a rebated edge reads the rebate.

Take the measurements on more than one door. A building rarely has one door type, and the second door is where you find out whether the first was representative or was the one somebody had already adjusted.

What "compatible" means in a quotation, and what it does not

The word does real damage in this category because it has no agreed definition and sounds like it does.

A quotation that says a product is compatible has usually established that it belongs to the same family, a mortise lock for a mortise pocket, a euro cylinder for a euro profile. That is a statement about category, not about the seven dimensions, and it is true of thousands of products that will not fit your door.

The phrase to use instead is a list. "Matches on backset, center distance, forend length and width, and spindle square; differs on forend corner radius, which is square rather than 6mm radiused; case size not measured." That sentence is longer, and it is the difference between a purchase decision and a hope.

If a supplier will not produce that sentence, the useful question is which of the seven they have measured. An honest answer to that is more valuable than a confident answer to the original question.

When a drop-in is not the right answer

Two situations where matching the old lock is the expensive choice rather than the safe one.

The old lock was itself a compromise: on a door that has been rehung, re-skinned or refinished, the installed lock is often a part somebody made fit. Copying it copies the compromise into every future order. Measure the door preparation, not the lock that is in it.

The opening is a fire door: a dimensionally identical replacement is still a change to a tested assembly unless the replacement is part of that assembly's scope. Our fire door page covers what that means; the short version is that interchangeability is a dimensional question and fire compliance is a documentary one, and passing the first does not answer the second.

The one-line summary

Interchangeable means seven dimensions match, not one. If a supplier answers the question with a single number, they have answered a different question, and the two they left out are the two that cost the most.

Questions this answers

Which dimensions decide whether a lock is interchangeable?

Seven: backset; center distance from spindle to cylinder; case size; forend length, width and corner radius; spindle square and follower; fixing centers; and door thickness range. The expensive failures cluster at the top of that list, and the top two are the ones people check least.

If the backset matches, will the lock fit?

Not necessarily. The backset locates only the spindle relative to the door edge. The center distance locates the cylinder relative to the spindle, and two locks can share a 60mm backset with different center distances, which puts the cylinder hole in the wrong place on a door already prepped for the other.

Why does the forend matter if the lock works?

Because it decides whether the installation looks planned. A replacement with a shorter forend leaves the old mortise visible above and below the new faceplate, on every door, at eye level. The corner radius matters too, a routed mortise has radiused corners and a chiselled one square, and a mismatch shows as four crescents or four squares.

How many of your locks publish these dimensions?

Of 258 lock products: backset 111 (43%), door thickness 77 (30%), center distance 35 (14%), forend 8 (3%), case size 6 (2%). The two dimensions that cause the most expensive failures are the two we publish least. A missing dimension can be measured on request: it is missing because nobody has measured that model yet, not because it is withheld.

What should I send to get a drop-in replacement?

A sample if possible. Otherwise: the existing make and model number, photographs of the door edge and face with a ruler in frame, the seven dimensions measured with a note of which you measured versus copied, the door thickness and construction, and the finish and function.

Is copying the existing lock always the right approach?

No. On a door that has been rehung, re-skinned or refinished, the installed lock is often a part somebody made fit, copying it copies the compromise into every future order. Measure the door preparation rather than the lock in it. And on a fire door, dimensional matching does not answer the certificate question.
