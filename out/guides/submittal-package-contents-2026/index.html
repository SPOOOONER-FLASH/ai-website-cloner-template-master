Door Hardware Submittal Package 2026: Every Document the Reviewer Needs, and the Four That Get It Rejected

A hardware submittal is rejected for the same handful of reasons on almost every project, and none of them is the hardware. This page lists what a complete package contains, names the four omissions that send it back, explains the difference between a product datasheet and evidence, sets out who signs what, and states plainly which of these documents we publish, which we supply on request, and which we do not have at all.

A submittal is not a sales document. It is the moment a specifier checks whether what was ordered is what was specified, and it is reviewed by somebody whose job is to find the gap rather than to be persuaded.

That distinction explains almost every rejected package. A supplier sends marketing material, because marketing material is what they have; a reviewer needs evidence, and sends it back. Two weeks pass. The same thing happens again.

This page is what goes in the package, in the order a reviewer reads it.

The shape of a complete package

A hardware submittal has five parts, and a reviewer looks for them in this order.

The schedule comes first because it is the index. A reviewer reads it to find out what the project is buying, then checks each line against the other four parts. A package without a schedule forces them to build one themselves, which is the most common reason a package sits unreviewed for a fortnight.

The hardware schedule, opening by opening

A schedule is organized by opening, not by product. Each opening carries a number that matches the drawings, a hardware set reference, and the full item list for that set.

Each line item needs the manufacturer, the catalog number, the finish code, the quantity, and the function where the product has one. A line that says "lever handle, SSS" has named a shape and a finish and left out the model, which is the information the whole document exists to carry.

The schedule is also where handing lives, and handing is the single most expensive thing to get wrong at this stage because it cannot be corrected on site for most products. State the convention you are using as well as the hand itself, our page on handing covers why the European and North American conventions can describe the same door with different words.

Product data: what a datasheet has to carry

A datasheet is the manufacturer's statement of what the product is. For a reviewer it has to answer four questions without a phone call.

What are its dimensions, in the units the specification uses. A specification written in inches and a datasheet published only in millimeters is a conversion the reviewer has to do and then take responsibility for, which is why our English pages carry both.

What finish is it, stated as a code rather than a color name. "Satin stainless" is a description; US32D / 630 is a code that two parties can check against the same standard.

What does it fit, door thickness range, backset, preparation type, glass thickness where relevant.

What is its function, where the product has functions. A lock model number without a function suffix is incomplete, and the reviewer cannot tell whether the storeroom door will lock behind somebody.

Test evidence is not product data, and the difference is the whole point

This is where most packages fail, and it fails because of a category confusion that looks reasonable from the supplier's side.

A datasheet is a statement by the manufacturer about their own product. A test certificate is a statement by an accredited third party about a specific tested configuration. A specification that asks for a fire rating, a durability grade or a corrosion class is asking for the second, and the first does not substitute for it however confidently it is written.

The practical test a reviewer applies: can I read the report number, the issuing laboratory, the standard, the date, the scope of application, and the exact configuration tested. If any of those is missing, the document is a claim rather than evidence.

A certificate for a similar product from the same range is also not evidence. Test results attach to what was tested, this is the same principle set out on our fire door hardware page, where substituting a same-size hinge takes the door outside its certificate.

The four omissions that get a package rejected

In rough order of how often they happen.

No finish codes, only finish names: a schedule saying "polished chrome" across forty openings is unverifiable. Two manufacturers' polished chrome are visibly different products, and the reviewer has no way to confirm the project gets the one specified. Our finish code reference covers the codes and why the same code from two suppliers can still differ.

Handing absent or stated without its convention: covered above. It is the most expensive of the four because the correction is usually new product rather than a resubmittal.

Test evidence replaced by a marketing claim: "Fire rated", "ANSI Grade 1", "UL listed" written next to a product with no certificate number behind it. A reviewer who catches one of these does not merely reject that line; they start checking the others, and the package's credibility is gone for the rest of the project.

No preparation information: the schedule names a mortise lock; the door manufacturer needs the pocket dimensions, the spindle and cylinder hole positions and the center distance. Our door preparation page sets out what "prepped for" does and does not guarantee, and the short version is that a preparation type is not a template.

Who signs what, and why it matters

A submittal carries responsibility as well as information, and the reviewer is checking that too.

The manufacturer stands behind the product data and the drawings. The accredited laboratory stands behind the test evidence. The supplier or hardware consultant stands behind the schedule, that the right product is on the right opening. The reviewer approves against the specification, which is not the same as approving the product's fitness for purpose.

A package where the supplier has written all of it, including a paraphrase of a test result, has collapsed three of those roles into one. That is exactly what a reviewer is trained to spot.

What we publish, what we send on request, and what we do not have

This is the part suppliers normally leave vague, so here it is as a table. It is the same inventory our documents page carries.

The last two rows are the ones worth reading carefully. We do not have BIM families and we do not have editable native CAD, and saying so here costs us fewer projects than discovering it three weeks into a submittal cycle costs you.

A supplier who answers "yes, we can provide BIM" and then sends a PDF renamed with a .rvt extension has not solved anything; they have moved the discovery to the point where a model is already being built around the assumption.

Two things that make a resubmittal unnecessary

Send the specification section to the factory before the quotation, not after the order. Almost every rejected package contains a product that was correctly quoted against an incomplete question. If the specification says EN 1634-1 EI 30, that constraint should reach the factory while a substitution is still free.

Ask for the evidence before you need it. A test report either exists or it does not, and finding out at submittal stage converts a documentation question into a program delay. Our page on what documents you can actually get lists what to ask for and how to read the answer.

Substitution requests, and why most of them are refused

A substitution request asks the specifier to accept a product other than the one named. It is a normal part of the process, and it is refused far more often than it needs to be for a reason that is entirely fixable.

A refused request almost always compares the wrong things. It asserts that the substitute is equivalent and then lists the features the two products share. What the specifier needs is the opposite: every respect in which they differ, and evidence that each difference is acceptable on this opening.

So a request that works states the specified product, the proposed product, and a line-by-line comparison of the properties the specification actually constrains (grade, cycles, fire suitability, corrosion class, finish code, dimensions) with the test evidence behind each one. Where a property is worse, say so, and say why it does not matter here.

A request that hides a difference is refused the moment the reviewer finds it, and every later request from the same supplier is read with that in mind. The shared features were never the question.

Where our own gaps are

Some of our products are in testing and do not yet hold certificates. Where that is true we say so on the product page rather than implying a rating, and a submittal that needs a certificate we do not hold is a submittal we will tell you we cannot complete.

That is a smaller number of lost inquiries than it sounds, and it is the only position compatible with the rest of this page. A supplier who will write an unsupported "fire rated" on a submittal will also write an unsupported dimension on a drawing, and the reviewer knows it.

Questions this answers

What goes in a door hardware submittal package?

Five parts, read in this order: the hardware schedule (the index, organized by opening), product data per catalog number, shop drawings with door and frame preparation, test evidence for anything the specification requires, and a keying schedule where the project has a key system.

What is the difference between a datasheet and test evidence?

A datasheet is the manufacturer's statement about their own product. A test certificate is an accredited third party's statement about a specific tested configuration. A specification asking for a fire rating, durability grade or corrosion class is asking for the second, and the first never substitutes for it.

Why do submittals get rejected?

Four things, in rough order: finish names instead of finish codes; handing absent or stated without naming the convention; a marketing claim standing in for a certificate number; and no preparation information for the door manufacturer. None of them is the hardware itself.

Can I use a certificate from a similar product in the same range?

No. Test results attach to the configuration that was tested, within the scope of application printed on the certificate. A certificate for a neighboring model is not evidence for this one, the same principle that makes substituting a same-size hinge take a fire door outside its certificate.

Do you supply BIM families or editable CAD?

No to both, and we would rather say so here than three weeks into a submittal cycle. We publish dimensioned drawings, specifications and the catalog; spec exports and test documents are available on request. BIM/Revit families and native editable CAD we do not have.

How do I avoid a resubmittal?

Send the specification section to the factory before the quotation rather than after the order, so a constraint like EN 1634-1 EI 30 arrives while a substitution is still free. And ask for test evidence early, a report either exists or it does not, and finding out at submittal stage turns a paperwork question into a program delay.
