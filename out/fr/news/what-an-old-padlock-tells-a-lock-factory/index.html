A Padlock Chained to a Knight in Germany

Our principal found it on a German street, chained around the ankles of a cast-iron knight outside a hotel, and photographed it because the older a thing is the more it draws you in. We cannot tell you who made it or when, rust removes exactly the evidence that would answer that. We can tell you what is still legible in the metal, which is more than you would expect, and why we are not going to invent the rest.

Our principal was walking down a street in Germany when he stopped at a hotel doorway. Outside it stands a cast knight in armour, holding a shield whose inscription claims the year 998 and names Richeza, Queen of Poland, granddaughter of the Emperor Otto II. Chained around the knight's ankles is a padlock, and it was the padlock he photographed. He sent the pictures to the company group with one line: put this on the website. The older something is, the more it pulls people in, and the more it shows we know what we are doing.

He has been cutting moulds for door hardware since 1998, so when he says an object is worth looking at, the right response is to look at it. Here is what we can see, and (because this matters more) here is where our seeing stops.

Start with how it is held together. Eight domed rivets ring the body, two at the shoulders, two down each flank, two across the foot. That is a case assembled from plates, not cast in one piece and not welded: cut, stacked, drilled, riveted cold, and the domes are the heads spread by a hammer. It is the construction you use when the joint has to hold without heat, and it is the reason the thing has survived a century of weather with its shape intact while its surface went to rust.

Then the keyhole, which is the most talkative part of the whole object. There is a pin standing in the middle of it. A pin means the key is hollow (a barrel key that slides over it and is centered by it) and a hollow key with a wide slot beneath means a warded lock: fixed obstructions inside that the right key is cut to pass and the wrong one is not. This is not a pin-tumbler cylinder and it was never meant to be one. It is the mechanism that secured Europe for several hundred years before cylinders replaced it, and you can identify it from two meters away once you know what the pin is for.

What that mechanism actually resisted

A ward is an obstruction, not a puzzle. The plates inside the case are cut so that a key of the right shape sweeps past them and a key of the wrong shape jams against them. Nothing inside measures anything. There is no stack of pins to be raised to a shear line, no lever to be lifted to a gate, only a path that is either clear or blocked.

That makes the mechanism very good at one thing and poor at another. It is almost impossible to jam with dirt, ice or paint, which is why locks of this kind hung on gates, sheds and outbuildings rather than on the front doors of banks, and it is a large part of why this one still turns after whatever it has been through. It is also defeated by a key with everything cut away except the part that throws the bolt. That key has a name everybody knows and almost nobody connects to its origin: a skeleton key is skeletal because the wards have been filed off it.

Three inventions ended the warded lock's career in serious work, and all three are on the public record with dates. Robert Barron's double-acting lever lock, patented in Britain in 1778, made the lock measure the key rather than merely admit it, every lever had to be lifted to its own height, neither under nor over. Jeremiah Chubb's detector lock of 1818 added a mechanism that seized if any lever was lifted too far, so an attempt on the lock left evidence behind. And Linus Yale Jr.'s pin-tumbler cylinder, patented in the United States in the 1860s, moved the measuring into a small separate cylinder that could be fitted into a case somebody else had made.

That last one matters more than it sounds, and it is the direct ancestor of the euro profile discussed further down this page. Once the measuring part is separable, the lock case stops being the security and becomes the housing, and the day the housing is standardized, any factory's cylinder can go into any factory's door.

Here is the honest part, and it cuts against the rest of this article. None of that dates the padlock in the photograph. Warded padlocks did not stop being made when better mechanisms arrived; they got cheaper and kept selling, and they are manufactured today for lockers, gates and toolboxes where the job is to keep an honest person honest rather than to stop a determined one. The mechanism tells you what is inside. It does not tell you what century you are holding.

Then the thing that has no security function at all. Across the upper body sits an oval plate in a brighter metal, engraved with a scalloped arc border, and two of the rivets pass straight through it. Somebody decorated a lock that was going to hang outdoors and get filthy. Nobody paid for that engraving because it made the lock harder to open, they paid for it because a lock is a thing people look at while they decide whether to trust what is behind it. We recognize the impulse. It is the same one that puts a consistent finish on a spec table.

Now the stop. We do not know who made this lock, in what year, or in what country. We do not know whether it is as old as the building it decorates or was bought in a market fifty years ago to dress a statue. Rust removes exactly the evidence that would answer those questions: the stamp, the plating, the paint, the paper. A shape like this was made by hundreds of workshops across Europe and Asia for a very long time, and no honest look at it narrows that down.

We could write the missing paragraph. A German workshop in the 1890s, a family name, a line about the smith who set those rivets, it would read beautifully, and it would be fiction. We are not going to, and the reason is not modesty. A buyer ordering a container of hardware from a factory they have never visited is buying one thing above all others: the belief that we do not make things up. That belief is indivisible. A supplier who will invent a provenance for a padlock on a blog will invent a backset for a lock case in a quotation, and the buyer who catches the first is right to assume the second. On this site, where a dimension is not published, you get a dash instead of a plausible number. This is the same rule applied to a story.

The lock is also interesting for the opposite reason to the one people expect. It is anonymous, and for most of the history of this trade that was normal: a lock was identified by the man who made it, and if you did not know him you could not buy a replacement part. What ended that was not better locks. It was standards. The profile that every European lock case is machined for today is defined by DIN 18252 in Germany and EN 1303 across Europe, to a tolerance that lets a cylinder from one factory drop into a body from another, in another country, forty years later. The country where our principal found this nameless lock is the same country that wrote the standard that made namelessness safe.

That is what we make. Our own euro-profile cylinders run 45, 54, 60, 65, 70, 80 and 90mm (3-9/16"), and not one of those figures is ours, they belong to the standard, and the entire value of the part is that we hit them exactly. A cylinder proudly two millimeters different is not a distinctive product; it is a part that does not fit. The anonymity of the old lock and the interchangeability of the new one are the same story told twice: first the maker's name was the guarantee, then the standard replaced it.

Which is where twenty-eight years actually goes. A mould for one exit device takes three to five months before it produces a part anyone would sell, and most of that is not cutting steel, it is running the tool in, measuring what comes out, and correcting the tool, because metal does not do what the drawing says it will. Then come the tests the part has to survive: fire, impact, cycle life, pressure. None of that shows up in a photograph. It shows up in the hundred-thousandth part matching the first.

If you ever want to read one yourself

Four things survive rust, roughly in that order, and one thing does not.

How the case is joined: rivets, a folded seam, a weld bead or a single cast shell. This is readable under almost any amount of corrosion because it is structure rather than surface, you can feel a rivet through rust that has hidden everything else. Rivets and folded seams mean the body was built up from plate.

The shackle's geometry: look at whether the bolt catches the shackle at one end or at both. Heel-and-toe locking (both legs held) is the stronger arrangement and the later one; a shackle held only at the heel can be swung clear if the free leg is cut or forced, which is why a cheap padlock and an expensive one can look identical from the front and behave nothing alike.

The keyhole: covered above, and the single most informative square centimeter on any old lock. A pin means a hollow key. A plain slot means a flat key. A keyway with a shaped profile means a cylinder, and a cylinder means the lock is younger than everything discussed here.

The decoration: engraving, a plate in a contrasting metal, a scalloped border. It tells you nothing at all about the mechanism and a great deal about who the thing was sold to.

What does not survive is the maker's stamp. A stamp is a shallow displacement of the surface (a few tenths of a millimeter) and surface is precisely what rust consumes first. If you find an old lock with a legible name on it, you have found the rare one, and it is the only one of these five that can actually answer where and when.

Which leads to the one piece of advice in this article. Do not take a wire brush to it. Abrasive cleaning removes the last tenths of the only evidence that answers the question everybody asks first. We would rather look at a rusty lock that can still be identified than a bright one that cannot, and that preference is not sentimental, it is the same instinct that makes us publish a dash instead of a guess.

For the record: we do not make padlocks. Canton Hyland makes door hardware (locks that go into a door rather than onto a chain) and there is no padlock anywhere in our catalog. This one is on the site because our principal photographed it in the street, not because it is a product.

So the padlock goes on this page as something to look at, not something to claim. It is not ours, we did not make it, and we cannot tell you who did. What we can tell you is how it was put together, what kind of key it takes, and which part of it was pure pride, and that we can read it at all is because we spend our days at the other end of the same object's life.
