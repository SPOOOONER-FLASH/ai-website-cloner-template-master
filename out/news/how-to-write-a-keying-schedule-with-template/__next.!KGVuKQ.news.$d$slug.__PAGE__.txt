37:["$","p","19",{"children":"Missing or ambiguous lengths. A row that says only 70mm has given half the answer. A euro cylinder is described by its overall length and by its split either side of the cam, and the split decides whether the cylinder sits right on both faces of this particular door. Two conventions add to the confusion. Our 70 SNKT-2 record writes its split as 30/10/30, two 30mm sections either side of a 10mm cam section; the common habit of measuring each side to the center of the cam writes the same cylinder as 35/35. 38:["$","p","20",{"children":"Mixing profiles. Every cylinder in one master-keyed system has to share a profile and a keyway, because a master key has to physically enter every cylinder it opens. A euro cylinder and an oval cylinder are not interchangeable, and neither fits the other's lock case. Our Single oval cylinder is a cylinder of the second kind: its record lists it as an external single cylinder, 30mm with extension sizes optional, 6-pin, supplied with 3 keys. It is a correct product for an oval lock case and an impossible one on a euro-profile schedule. This mistake usually comes from a schedule assembled out of two lock suppliers' catalogs, so check the lock-case column against the profile column before you assign a single symbol."}]
39:["$","p","21",{"children":"Cross-keying that eats capacity. Each cross-keyed cylinder answers to keys from more than one branch, which means extra shear points, which removes combinations the rest of the system could have used. Granted one door at a time, it adds up until no further unique change keys can be cut. The defense on the schedule is simple: mark every cross-keyed row with an X, name the second key in the notes, and count the X rows before the schedule goes out.
