200,000 Cycles: What the LH Lever Range Is Actually Rated For

LH852, LH853, LH855, 808 and 6491 share a published cycle life of 200,000 operations, a 60/70mm backset, a 35–45mm (1-3/8" to 1-3/4") adjustable door thickness and a solid steel zinc-plated chassis behind a zinc-alloy lever. What separates them is the function set and the shape, and only one of the five offers a dummy.

Five lever sets in our selected range — LH852, LH853, LH855, 808 and 6491 — publish the same cycle life: 200,000 operations. That number is the most useful thing on their specification sheets and also the most easily misread, so it is worth being precise about what it describes and what it does not.

A cycle is one full operation: lever down, latch retracted, lever released, latch returned. Two hundred thousand of them is a service expectation for a door in normal use, not a warranty and not a grade in the ANSI sense — A156 grades exit devices and locks on cycle counts, and we hold no ANSI/BHMA certification, so this figure is our own published expectation rather than a graded result. Stating it that way is the difference between a specification and an advertisement.

What makes the number credible is the construction behind it, and that is published too. The lever itself is zinc alloy. The chassis is solid steel, zinc plated for corrosion resistance. That division is deliberate: the lever is the visible part and zinc casts into shapes steel cannot, while the chassis is the part that carries the load every one of those 200,000 times, and it is steel. A lever set that is zinc all the way through wears at the spindle, and it does it long before the finish shows any sign of it.

The dimensions are shared across all five: a 60/70mm backset and a door thickness of 35mm (1-3/8") to 45mm (1-3/4") adjustable, with the LH853 also published at 60mm (2-3/8") on some variants. All five are fully reversible, left or right hand, so handing is an assembly matter rather than a separate part number — which is worth knowing when a schedule arrives with the handing column empty, as they often do.

Where they separate is function. The LH855 is published with the widest set: entrance, privacy, passage or dummy. A dummy is a lever fixed to the door with no mechanism at all, used on the inactive leaf of a pair or on a cupboard, and it is the one function buyers routinely forget to order and then improvise on site with a working set. If your schedule has inactive leaves on it, that is the model to ask about first.

The 808 is the one to look at where the finish is doing the work. It is published in antique brass (US5) and matt black, and antique brass is a relieved finish — colour worked back off the high points — rather than a plating with one uniform tone. That is why it is not translatable to a single BHMA number, as our finish-code article explains, and also why it looks different on a lever than on a flat rose. Order a sample of the actual lever rather than a finish chip.

All five are published as keyable alike with deadbolts, or master keyed. That is the row that turns a lever range into a project specification: the lever set, the deadbolt above it and the cylinder in the door next to it can be one keying group, which is what a building manager is actually buying. The number of levels that group needs is a separate decision and usually a smaller one than the enquiry assumes.

The trim material is published as zinc die-casting electro-plated, or solid brass. That is a genuine choice rather than a grade — solid brass is the option where the door is exterior or coastal, and the electro-plated die casting is the option everywhere else. Say which one you want at enquiry, because it is not a finish decision and cannot be changed by picking a different finish code.

## Two kinds of lever in one category

The five lever sets above belong to one of two quite different kinds of product that share the name "lever handle" in our catalogue, and ordering the wrong kind is a more expensive mistake than ordering the wrong finish.

The first kind is a **tubular lever set** — what LH852, LH853, LH855, 808 and 6491 are. It carries its own latch in a bored hole, which is why its specification talks about backset, cycle life, chassis and function: the lever set is the whole lock. About half of our lever-handle entries are of this kind, and they share the rows described above — a 60/70mm backset, a 35–45mm door, 200,000 cycles, a solid steel chassis and full reversibility.

The second kind is a **lever on a rose or a plate**, made to be paired with a separate mortise lock case. It has no latch of its own; the lock case in the door edge does that work, and the lever only turns it. Its specification talks about different things: which lock case it is matched to, the rose diameter or plate size, the projection and the lever length. On our records the lock-case row reads 8550 / 7255 on most of them, CT60 / 7255 on some, and CF60 on the lift-to-lock versions.

The practical test is simple. If the door is bored with a round hole through its face and a smaller one into its edge, it takes a tubular set. If it has a rectangular pocket cut into the edge for a lock body, it takes a lever on rose or plate, and the lock case is a separate line on the order.

## The door range is different too

Because the two kinds fix differently, they take different leaf thicknesses. The tubular sets are published for 35–45mm (1-3/8" to 1-3/4") adjustable. The mortise levers follow their lock case: those matched to the 8550 / 7255 case are published for 38–50mm (1-1/2" to 1-15/16"), those on the CT60 / 7255 case for 36–56mm or 40–58mm, and the CF60 lift-to-lock versions for 40–58mm (1-9/16" to 2-5/16") — the ranges to look at for a heavy timber or a fire door thicker than a standard internal leaf. Check the leaf against the lever you are actually ordering, not against the range of the category.

## Rose diameter, and why it matters on a replacement

On the mortise levers, the rose diameter is published — most often Ø50mm or Ø52mm (about 2"), and on a few models Ø59mm or Ø62mm. On a new door that is mostly an appearance choice. On a replacement it is a practical one: a new rose has to cover the marks, screw holes and paint line left by the old hardware. A larger rose is the easiest way to hide a previous installation, so measure what the old rose covered before choosing the new one.

## Projection and lever length: the numbers that meet the wall

Two more rows on the mortise levers are the ones that decide what the lever hits. **Projection** — how far the lever stands out from the door face — is published between about 52mm and 69mm (2" to 2-3/4"). **Lever length** runs from about 130mm to 157mm (5-1/8" to 6-3/16").

Projection is the figure to carry across to the rest of the door. It sets how much clearance the lever needs from a return wall when the door is fully open, and it is the number our article on door stops tells you to choose a stop against: a stop shorter than the lever's projection lets the lever reach the wall first. Lever length matters on a narrow door or beside a return, where a long lever can foul the frame or a hand can be trapped between lever and wall.

## Lift-to-lock

One function on the mortise side deserves its own mention because it behaves differently from everything else in the range. The versions matched to the CF60 case are published as lift-to-lock: lifting the lever above horizontal throws the locking, and the key then secures it. In the usual arrangement it goes with multi-point locking on residential entrance doors, and it only works with the lock body it was designed for. A lift-to-lock lever ordered for a conventional mortise case, or the reverse, will fit the door and fail to operate the lock, so the lock case row matters more on these than on any other lever.

## Plates: when the lever and the cylinder share a backing

Some levers in the category are published on plates rather than roses — square plates of 52 × 52mm, and long plates of 160 × 35mm and 167.5 × 32mm among them. A small square plate behaves like a rose. A long plate is a different thing: it usually carries the lever and the cylinder hole together on one backing, which means the distance between the two holes on the plate has to match the distance between the spindle and the cylinder in the lock case behind it. Get that pairing wrong and the plate covers the door but the cylinder hole sits in the wrong place. The lock-case row on each lever is the check, and our article on mortise lock backset and centre distance explains the measurement.

One honest gap to finish on. Thirty entries in the lever-handle category publish neither a lock-case row nor a cycle-life row, so from the page alone you cannot tell which of the two kinds they are. Rather than guess from a photograph, ask — tell us whether your door has a bored hole or a mortise pocket, and we will tell you which of those thirty fit it.

For a quotation on this range, four rows are enough: function per door, backset, leaf thickness, and whether any of it is keyed together. The cycle figure is the same across all five, so it will not choose between them; the function set and the trim material will.

Questions this answers

What does a 200,000 cycle rating mean on a lever handle?

A cycle is one full operation — lever down, latch retracted, lever released, latch returned — so it is a service expectation for a door in normal use. Why is the lever zinc alloy but the chassis steel?

Because they do different jobs. Which lever model covers a dummy or inactive leaf?

The LH855, which is published with the widest function set: entrance, privacy, passage or dummy. Is the trim material a finish choice?

No, and it cannot be changed by picking a different finish code. The trim is published as zinc die-casting electro-plated, or solid brass, and that is a genuine choice rather than a grade: solid brass is the option where the door is exterior or coastal, and the electro-plated die casting is the option everywhere else. What is the difference between a tubular lever set and a lever on rose?

A tubular lever set carries its own latch in a bored hole, so its specification covers backset, cycle life and function — the lever set is the whole lock. A lever on rose or plate has no latch; it turns a separate mortise lock case in the door edge, and its specification names the lock case it matches (on our records 8550/7255, CT60/7255 or CF60), plus rose size, projection and lever length. A round bored hole means tubular; a rectangular pocket in the edge means mortise.

How far does a lever handle project from the door?

On our mortise levers, projection is published between about 52mm and 69mm (2 to 2-3/4 inches), with lever lengths of roughly 130–157mm. Carry the projection across to the rest of the door: it sets the clearance needed from a return wall when the door is open, and a door stop should be at least as long as the lever's projection, or the lever will reach the wall first.
