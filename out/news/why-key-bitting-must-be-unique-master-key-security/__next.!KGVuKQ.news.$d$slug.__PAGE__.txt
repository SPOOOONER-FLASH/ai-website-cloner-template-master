2c:["$","p","7",{"children":"Near-duplicates are the subtler problem. Keys and cylinders are made to tolerances, and both wear. Two bittings that differ by a single depth step at a single position may be distinct on paper and still close enough in service for a worn key, or a worn cylinder, to bridge the difference. It costs capacity, and it is what keeps keyed different meaning different after years of use."}]
2d:["$","p","8",{"children":"Repeats across customers come from a different source. Low-cost keyed-different cylinders are often cut from a limited series of bittings that is reused from batch to batch. Each cylinder in your shipment may be unique within the shipment and still share its key with a cylinder in somebody else's shipment on the same keyway. For a single house door that may be an accepted trade-off. For a building with a hierarchy, a register and staff who leave, it is not, because a repeat outside the system is a key the system can never recall."}]
2e:["$","h2","incidental-master-keys",{"id":"incidental-master-keys","data-caps":false,"children":"Incidental master keys"}]
2f:["$","p","10",{"children":"A master-keyed cylinder works by adding a master pin, a short spacer, to some of its pin stacks. Each of those stacks now has two gaps that can sit at the shear line, one for the change key and one for the master key. The cylinder does not check which key each depth came from. If one position accepts depth A or depth B and the next accepts depth C or depth D, the cylinder accepts every mixture: A with D, B with C, and so on across every mastered position. Only two of those combinations were intended. The others are incidental master keys: valid keys that nobody designed and nobody cut, but that the cylinder will accept. Every extra master level multiplies them."}]
30:["$","p","11",{"children":"Incidental masters matter for two reasons. One of them may coincide with a change key issued elsewhere in the system, so a person holding a legitimate key for one door finds it opens a door it was never meant to. And more shear points give a cylinder more ways to be operated without the intended key, which is why a master-keyed cylinder is generally regarded as less resistant to manipulation than the same cylinder keyed different. Designers manage both with recognized progression methods, such as the rotating constant method and total position progression, and by limiting how many positions are mastered. A competent designer checks every issued key against every cylinder it should not open before anything is pinned."}]

32:["$","p","13",{"children":"MACS stands for maximum adjacent cut specification. It is the largest difference in depth the manufacturer allows between two neighboring cuts. A very deep cut beside a very shallow one leaves a steep, thin ridge of metal on the blade. Beyond the limit the key is weak at that point and can crack, the pin cannot ride smoothly up the slope, and the key drags or binds on the way in and out."}]

A bitting list produced by hand that ignores it gives keys that work on day one and fail first on the busiest doors. Ask whether bittings are generated by software against the platform's own specification, and whether the same software checks for duplicates and incidental masters."}]
