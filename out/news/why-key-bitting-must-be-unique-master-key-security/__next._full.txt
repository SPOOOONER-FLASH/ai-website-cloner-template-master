5a:["$","p","7",{"children":"Near-duplicates are the subtler problem. Keys and cylinders are made to tolerances, and both wear. 5d:["$","p","10",{"children":"A master-keyed cylinder works by adding a master pin, a short spacer, to some of its pin stacks. Each of those stacks now has two gaps that can sit at the shear line, one for the change key and one for the master key. The cylinder does not check which key each depth came from. If one position accepts depth A or depth B and the next accepts depth C or depth D, the cylinder accepts every mixture: A with D, B with C, and so on across every mastered position. Only two of those combinations were intended. The others are incidental master keys: valid keys that nobody designed and nobody cut, but that the cylinder will accept. Every extra master level multiplies them."}]
5e:["$","p","11",{"children":"Incidental masters matter for two reasons. Designers manage both with recognized progression methods, such as the rotating constant method and total position progression, and by limiting how many positions are mastered. A competent designer checks every issued key against every cylinder it should not open before anything is pinned."}]
