200,000 Cycles: What a Durability Figure Means in EN 1906 and in ANSI/BHMA, and Why It Is Not the Same Number

We publish 200,000 cycles on 132 models. In EN 1906 that is the top durability category. In ANSI/BHMA A156.2 it is the entry grade. Both statements are true, and a specification that reads one as the other will either over-buy or under-buy. This page sets out what a cycle test actually is, what each scale asks for, and where our own figure sits on both.

A durability figure on a datasheet is a single number, which is what makes it dangerous. Two hundred thousand cycles is the top of one scale and the bottom of another, and nothing on the datasheet says which scale the number was measured against.

We publish 200,000 cycles on 132 of our models. This page says what that number is, where it sits on each of the two systems a specifier might be reading it against, and what it does not cover.

WHAT A CYCLE IS

One cycle is one complete operation of the mechanism: the handle or key moves, the latch retracts, the mechanism returns. A test rig performs that operation repeatedly, under a stated load, until either the target count is reached or the product fails a defined criterion, the latch no longer retracts fully, the return spring no longer returns it, a component fractures.

What makes a cycle count meaningful is the definition of failure attached to it, not the count. A figure quoted without its standard is a number without a test behind it, and a number without a test can be anything.

THE EUROPEAN SCALE

EN 1906 classifies lever handles and knob furniture across eight positions, one of which is durability. In that position the scale has two entries that matter in practice: category 6 at 100,000 cycles, and category 7 at 200,000 cycles.

Category 7 is the top of the durability position in that standard. A handle set tested to 200,000 cycles under EN 1906 has met the highest durability class the standard defines for that product.

The other seven positions in the EN 1906 code cover category of use, door mass, fire suitability, safety, corrosion resistance, security and type of operation. Durability is one eighth of the classification, which is why quoting only the cycle figure from an EN classification discards most of what was tested.

THE NORTH AMERICAN SCALE

ANSI/BHMA grades the whole product as Grade 1, 2 or 3. For bored locks under A156.2 the cycle requirements are 1,000,000 for Grade 1, 400,000 for Grade 2 and 200,000 for Grade 3. Mortise locks under A156.13 set Grade 1 at 1,000,000.

On that scale 200,000 cycles is Grade 3, the entry level. The same number that tops the European durability position sits at the bottom of the American grade ladder.

This is not a discrepancy to be resolved. It is two standards asking different questions of different product categories, with different assumptions about how a door in that market is used and how long the hardware is expected to stay in service before replacement.

WHY THE TWO SCALES DIVERGE SO FAR

The North American grading system was built around commercial openings where the lockset is expected to outlast several tenancies and where the replacement cost includes the labour of a locksmith on a Grade 1 mortise. The European system grades a handle set as furniture on a door whose lock case is a separately classified item, so the durability demanded of the handle is not the durability demanded of the lock.

The practical consequence: a European classification and an American grade are not comparable even when the cycle numbers are identical, because they were counted on different assemblies.

WHERE OUR FIGURE SITS, STATED BOTH WAYS

Our 200,000 cycle figure appears on 132 records in our catalog, 85 of them on published product pages. Read against EN 1906 it corresponds to the top durability category. Read against ANSI/BHMA A156.2 it corresponds to the Grade 3 cycle requirement.

We publish both readings because publishing one would be selecting the flattering half. A specifier writing to a European standard and a specifier writing to an American one are both entitled to place the number correctly, and only one of those two readings helps us.

WHAT THE CYCLE COUNT DOES NOT COVER

Corrosion. A cycle test runs in a laboratory, not in salt air. Corrosion resistance is a separate classification under EN 1670 and a separate part of the ANSI/BHMA suite, and a high cycle count on a finish that fails in two coastal winters is a product that stops working for a reason the cycle test never looked at.

Security. Cycle testing measures wear under normal operation, not resistance to attack. That is EN 1303 territory for cylinders and a different test entirely.

Fire. A cycle count says nothing about whether the assembly holds in a furnace. That is EN 1634-1, UL 10C or NFPA 252, and it is tested on a complete door assembly rather than on the hardware alone.

WHAT WE DO NOT PUBLISH

Which standard each 200,000 figure was tested against is not stated on every one of the 132 records. Where a test report exists we publish its facts (laboratory, reference number, issue date and the model covered) and send the document itself through the proper route.

Where a record carries the cycle figure without a named standard, treat it as a manufacturer's stated figure rather than as a third-party classification, and ask before writing it into a submittal. That distinction is the difference between a number a specifier can defend and one they cannot, and it is worth the email.

Questions this answers

What does 200,000 cycles mean on a door hardware datasheet?

That the mechanism completed 200,000 full operations on a test rig before either reaching the target or failing a defined criterion. The number is only meaningful with the standard it was measured against, because the same figure is the top category in EN 1906 and the entry grade in ANSI/BHMA A156.2.

Is 200,000 cycles good or bad?

Both, depending on the scale. Under EN 1906 it is category 7, the highest durability class that standard defines for handle sets. Under ANSI/BHMA A156.2 it is the Grade 3 requirement, with Grade 2 at 400,000 and Grade 1 at 1,000,000.

What are the ANSI/BHMA cycle requirements by grade?

For bored locks under A156.2: 1,000,000 cycles for Grade 1, 400,000 for Grade 2, 200,000 for Grade 3. Mortise locks under A156.13 set Grade 1 at 1,000,000. The grade covers more than cycles, but the cycle count is the figure most often quoted from it.

Why do the European and American cycle numbers differ so much?

They grade different things. ANSI/BHMA grades a complete lockset for a commercial opening expected to outlast several tenancies. EN 1906 grades the handle set as furniture on a door whose lock case is separately classified, so the durability asked of the handle is not the durability asked of the lock.

Does a high cycle count mean the finish will last?

No. A cycle test runs in a laboratory and measures mechanical wear. Corrosion resistance is classified separately, under EN 1670 in Europe, and a product can pass 200,000 cycles and still lose its finish in two coastal winters.

Is your 200,000 figure third-party certified?

Not on every one of the 132 records that carry it. Where a test report exists we publish its laboratory, reference number, issue date and the specific model covered, and send the document through the proper route. Where a record states the figure without a named standard, treat it as a manufacturer's stated figure and ask before writing it into a submittal.
