Fire Door Hardware 2026: Which Components Must Be Rated, EN 1634-1 vs UL 10C and NFPA 252, and What a Certificate Covers

A fire door is not a product; it is a tested assembly, and every item fixed to it is part of what was tested. This page lists the components that must be rated, explains the difference between EN 1634-1 and UL 10C / NFPA 252 including the hose stream test that only one of them has, sets out what a certificate actually covers, and states plainly which of our own products do and do not carry a fire rating today.

The most expensive misunderstanding in this industry is the belief that a fire door is a door. It is not. It is an assembly (leaf, frame, seals, hinges, lock, closer, and every fixing) that was tested together and passed together, and the certificate describes that assembly rather than any one part of it.

Everything difficult about fire door hardware follows from that single sentence.

WHICH COMPONENTS HAVE TO BE RATED

Anything that is fixed to the door, cut into the door, or that the door's performance depends on.

Hinges. They hold the leaf in the frame while it distorts under heat, and a hinge that fails drops the leaf out of the opening. Suitability for fire use is a field in the EN 1935 classification and is not a property of the material.

The lock or latch. A fire door works by staying closed; a leaf held only by a closer against a warped frame is not a barrier. The latch has to keep engaging the strike as the assembly moves.

The closer. It is what returns the door to the closed position, and a fire door propped open or left ajar performs as an opening rather than as a door.

Intumescent and smoke seals. These expand under heat to close the gaps, and they are the component most often disturbed by later work, a lock fitted after handover can cut straight through a seal groove.

Exit devices and any panic hardware. These are both life-safety and fire components and are among the least interchangeable products in any catalog.

Glazing and glazing beads, where the door has a vision panel. The glass, the bead, the seal and the fixing method are all part of the tested configuration.

Flush bolts, coordinators and selectors on pairs. A pair of fire doors has to close in the correct order, and the coordinator is what makes that happen.

The fixings. Screws and through-bolts are part of the assembly, and a fixing substituted for a similar-looking one is a change to what was tested.

Anything else cut into the leaf, letter plates, air transfer grilles, cable transfers, electric strikes. Each is a hole in a barrier, and each needs to have been in the test or to have its own rated treatment.

EN 1634-1 AND THE NORTH AMERICAN STANDARDS

EN 1634-1 is the European fire resistance test for door and shutter assemblies. A result is expressed as a period in minutes with a letter classification (E for integrity, EI for integrity plus insulation, EW for integrity plus radiation) so EI 30 and E 30 are different results for the same duration.

That distinction is the one most often lost in translation. Integrity means the assembly stops flame and hot gas passing. Insulation means it also limits the temperature rise on the unexposed face. A door specified as EI when only E was tested is a specification that has not been met.

UL 10C and NFPA 252 are the corresponding North American tests. UL 10C is the positive pressure test, and the detail worth knowing is the hose stream test: after the fire exposure, the assembly is hit with a pressurised water jet. It tests whether what survived the fire also survives the impact and thermal shock of firefighting, and it has no direct counterpart in EN 1634-1.

This is why an EN result and a UL listing are not translations of each other. They test overlapping but not identical things, and no supplier can convert one into the other. A building in a jurisdiction that requires UL 10C needs a UL listing, not an EN classification with a favorable comparison attached.

WHAT A CERTIFICATE ACTUALLY COVERS

It covers the configuration that was tested, within the scope of application stated on it. That scope is the part people skip, and it is where almost all real-world non-compliance originates.

A certificate states a leaf size range, a frame type, a hardware set, a glazing arrangement and a set of permitted variations. Outside that scope, the certificate says nothing, not that the assembly fails, but that it was not tested and no claim is supported.

So: substituting a hinge of the same size and a different classification takes the door outside its certificate. Adding a vision panel that was not in the test takes it outside. Fitting a closer with a different mounting takes it outside. Using a longer screw takes it outside if the fixing was part of the tested set.

None of these substitutions necessarily makes a door unsafe. All of them make it undocumented, and at an inspection an undocumented fire door and a failed one are treated identically, because there is no evidence to distinguish them.

FIELD LABELS AND THE RECORD

A fire door that has been altered on site loses the basis of its original certification, and in many jurisdictions the route back is a field label or an equivalent re-certification by a competent person, which is a survey and a cost rather than a form.

The practical discipline is documentary: whoever changes anything on a fire door records what was changed and against what evidence. A building whose fire doors have been maintained for ten years without that record has a hundred doors that each need surveying to prove something everybody already believes.

WHAT WE CARRY, STATED PLAINLY

This is where a supplier's page normally becomes vague, so we will be specific.

Where one of our products has been tested as part of a fire-rated assembly and a certificate exists, the product page states the standard, the classification and the scope, and the certificate is available on request.

Where a product has not been tested, the page says nothing about fire performance, and that silence is deliberate. Our 307 and 311 exit devices are in testing. Until certificate numbers exist, nothing we publish will imply that they hold a fire rating, an ANSI grade or a UL listing, not in a product description, not in a comparison, and not by putting a standard's number near a product that has not been tested to it.

We also do not reproduce another manufacturer's certificate, listing or grade to describe our own product. A certificate belongs to the assembly it was issued for, and borrowing one is the same category of error as inventing a dimension.

THE FIVE QUESTIONS TO ASK ANY SUPPLIER

Which standard, and which classification, EI or E, and how many minutes; or UL 10C, and what listing number.

What is the scope of application on the certificate, and does our door fall inside it.

Which exact hardware set was in the tested assembly.

Can we see the certificate itself, not a summary of it.

If the answer to any of these is a comparison to another product, treat that as a no. A fire rating is a document or it is nothing.

Questions this answers

Which door hardware components have to be fire rated?

Everything fixed to the door, cut into it, or that its performance depends on: hinges, the lock or latch, the closer, intumescent and smoke seals, exit devices, glazing and beads, flush bolts and coordinators on pairs, the fixings themselves, and anything else cut into the leaf such as letter plates or air transfer grilles.

What is the difference between EI 30 and E 30?

E is integrity, the assembly stops flame and hot gas passing. EI adds insulation, meaning it also limits the temperature rise on the unexposed face. They are different results for the same duration, so a door specified as EI when only E was tested has not met the specification.

How does UL 10C differ from EN 1634-1?

UL 10C includes a hose stream test: after the fire exposure the assembly is hit with a pressurised water jet, testing whether it also survives the impact and thermal shock of firefighting. EN 1634-1 has no direct counterpart. The two are not translations of each other and no supplier can convert one into the other.

What does a fire certificate actually cover?

The configuration that was tested, within the scope of application printed on it, a leaf size range, a frame type, a hardware set, a glazing arrangement and permitted variations. Outside that scope the certificate says nothing: not that the door fails, but that no claim is supported.

Can I substitute a hinge of the same size on a fire door?

Not without taking the door outside its certificate. Fire suitability is a field in the EN 1935 classification, not a property of the material, and the hinges were part of the tested assembly. The substitution may well be safe; it is undocumented, and at inspection an undocumented fire door and a failed one are treated the same way.

Do your exit devices carry a fire rating?

Our 307 and 311 exit devices are in testing. Until certificate numbers exist, nothing we publish will imply they hold a fire rating, an ANSI grade or a UL listing. Where a product has been tested, the page states the standard, the classification and the scope, and the certificate is available on request.
